# On Living

I've always thought of myself as a person of contradictions.

I long for a peaceful, unhurried life, yet I am almost constantly anxious about the future. I love life, yet I often find myself doing things I don't really believe in. For as long as I can remember, there has always been another challenge waiting just ahead, and none of them has seemed easy. I finish one task, catch my breath for a moment, and soon find myself preparing for the next stage.

We seem remarkably good at overlooking what we already have while longing for something higher, something further away. I've tried to console myself with the thought that, from the perspective of a few years ago, I am already living a life I once wished for. But somehow, that thought has never quite persuaded me.

Perhaps the reason is simple: what I have achieved today offers no guarantee about tomorrow. Past successes are worth celebrating, but they cannot erase the uncertainty of what lies ahead. So I keep trying to become more capable, hoping that one day I will reach a point where I no longer have to worry so much about what comes next.

That doesn't mean I don't know how to enjoy the present. Exercise, entertainment, or a good trip can all take my mind off the future for a while. But when the happiness fades, the questions I was worried about are still there. In that sense, these moments can feel more like a temporary escape than a solution. Still, I don't think there's anything wrong with that. The happiness is real, at least. We cannot spend every moment pushing ourselves so hard.

There is another contradiction in me: I think of myself as resilient, yet also rather vulnerable.

When I truly believe in what I'm doing, I can stay focused for a remarkably long time. There are days when almost every waking hour goes into research. And when that effort is met with encouraging results, the sense of fulfillment is hard to replace.

But I've also been through long stretches of frustration. For nearly a year, my research made almost no progress. It was exhausting in a way that went beyond the work itself. More than once, I questioned my abilities and wondered whether I was really suited to research.

Looking back, however, that period became one of the most important experiences I've had.

Gradually, I came to see that failure does not necessarily mean a lack of ability. Outcomes depend on many things, and not every disappointment needs to be explained as a personal shortcoming. That change in perspective has made me more composed in the research I've done since. I think some of my recent progress owes something to it.

I consider myself resilient not because setbacks leave me untouched, but because, after many failures, I have never completely given up. I get anxious. I feel discouraged. I doubt myself. Yet some determination always remains, enough to make me willing to try again.

And still, I am vulnerable.

When I face judgment from others, whether favorable or unfavorable, I find it difficult to remain genuinely unshaken. I hope that someday my understanding of my own work will no longer change so readily with outside evaluations. Success is worth being happy about, and failure deserves reflection, but neither should replace my own judgment.

At this stage of my life, I am still a long way from that kind of steadiness.

There is yet another contradiction: my ambitions are large, but the life I ultimately want seems rather ordinary.

Of course I hope my research can create something of value. If one day my work genuinely advances a field, or even contributes something to society and to humanity, I would be deeply glad. Life is brief, and leaving something worthwhile behind would be a meaningful achievement.

But when I look carefully at my own motives, I have to admit that my personal life still comes first in my thinking.

So many people spend their days struggling simply to make a living. Before pursuing any grand ideal, I want to find material comfort and personal fulfillment, and to give myself and my future family a life that feels secure and free. If I still have the ability and enthusiasm to contribute to something larger after that, I would gladly do so.

I don't see these desires as incompatible. A person can hold ambitious ideals and still be honest about wanting a happy life of their own.

When I was little, I used to imagine my adult life. I wanted to become a scientist, do sophisticated research, earn a great deal of money, and live a life that others might envy.

Sometimes I think that, to the child I was then, the person I am now might already resemble that imagined future, at least a little.

What is interesting is that I still picture the future. Only the picture has changed.

It is a sunny afternoon. I'm sitting on the balcony of my own villa, looking out at the scenery. My wife is beside me, and we talk idly about where we might travel next. Our child is playing inside. Life is quiet and easy. There is no urgent deadline waiting, no next stage for which I have to prepare.

Some people might find it funny that someone barely in his twenties is already imagining a life that looks a little like retirement.

But as I've grown older, I've come to appreciate just how fortunate it would be to have a life whose direction I could truly choose for myself.

What I want is not necessarily to stop working, but to no longer *have* to work. Not to give up pursuing things, but to be free to decide what is worth pursuing. I could devote myself to research, or decide one day to take a trip. I could throw myself into a project, or stop when it feels like enough, without constantly worrying about what that choice might cost me.

I don't know whether reaching that point would really free me from the anxiety I feel today. I haven't lived that life yet. But for now, I tend to believe that having enough freedom to choose would help me feel more at ease.

None of this means that I intend to treat the coming decades as one long exercise in endurance.

I don't want to spend my life thinking that it will finally begin once I enter a certain university, publish a certain paper, find a good job, or earn enough money.

If someone is always waiting for happiness in the future, then even if the dream eventually comes true, it is hard to say whether the years spent waiting were lived well.

So while I work toward the future, I still want to take the present seriously. Research itself brings me moments of joy, and there are many other things in life worth cherishing. Even if some happiness is fleeting, even if tomorrow brings fresh difficulties, that does not make those moments any less meaningful.

There is a saying I am especially fond of:

**乘兴而去，兴尽而归。**

*To set out when the spirit moves you, and to return when the journey feels complete.*

To me, this doesn't mean acting on every passing impulse, or giving up as soon as things become difficult.

It means hoping to preserve a certain freedom: to begin something because I genuinely want to, to give it my full effort, and, if a day comes when it feels like enough, to choose another way of living without regret.

Perhaps I will always find new things to pursue. Or perhaps, after achieving some of my goals, I will gradually let go of things that matter so much to me today.

I don't know the answer yet.

But wherever I end up, I hope that when I look back, I will feel that most of my choices were truly my own, rather than steps taken under the pressure of expectations that could never be satisfied.

If I can say that, I think this journey will have been worth taking.
